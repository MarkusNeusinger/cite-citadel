Mary Bennet is a Bennet daughter described as "the only plain one in the family," who "worked hard
for knowledge and accomplishments" but had "neither genius nor taste," giving her performances a
"pedantic air and conceited manner."[^s1] She is "deep in the study of thorough bass and human
nature," offers moral observations such as a set-piece distinction between pride and vanity, and
reads extracts aloud.[^s2]

At the Netherfield ball she insists on singing a second song after her first is barely tolerated,
until [Mr. Bennet](mr-bennet.md) publicly stops her.[^s3] After Elizabeth refuses
[William Collins](william-collins.md)'s proposal, [Mrs. Bennet](mrs-bennet.md) briefly hopes Mary
might be prevailed on to accept him instead, since Mary rates his abilities more highly than her
sisters do.[^s4]

On Lydia's elopement, Mary offers Elizabeth a set-piece moral reflection that "loss of virtue in a
female is irretrievable" and that a woman's reputation is "no less brittle than it is
beautiful."[^s5]

On Jane's engagement to Bingley, Mary petitions to use the library at Netherfield.[^s6] By the
novel's close she is the only daughter remaining at home, drawn away from her studies by
[Mrs. Bennet](mrs-bennet.md)'s inability to sit alone; she still moralizes over every visitor, and,
no longer compared unfavourably to her sisters' beauty, is suspected by her father of accepting the
change without much reluctance.[^s7]
