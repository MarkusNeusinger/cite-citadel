William Collins is [Mr. Bennet](mr-bennet.md)'s cousin and, under the entail, heir to
[Longbourn](../objects/longbourn.md).[^s1] He writes to propose a visit, explaining he has been
ordained and given the rectory of Hunsford, Kent, by the patronage of
[Lady Catherine de Bourgh](lady-catherine-de-bourgh.md), and offering to make amends to his cousins for
inheriting their father's estate.[^s2] The narrator describes him, on arrival, as "a tall,
heavy-looking young man of five-and-twenty," grave, stately, and formal.[^s3]

He is obsequious toward Lady Catherine, describing her attentions and Rosings Park at exhaustive
length, and refuses to read novels aloud, choosing Fordyce's Sermons instead.[^s4] He first fixes on
[Jane Bennet](jane-bennet.md) as a prospective wife, then switches to
[Elizabeth Bennet](elizabeth-bennet.md) after Mrs. Bennet hints Jane is likely to be engaged.[^s5]
Elizabeth refuses his proposal decisively; within days he proposes to and is accepted by
[Charlotte Lucas](charlotte-lucas.md).[^s6]

At Hunsford, Collins shows the party his garden and the view of Rosings with minute, exhaustive
pride, and boasts of dining there twice weekly at her ladyship's invitation, never allowed to walk
home.[^s7] He is overjoyed when Lady Catherine invites the whole party, including Sir William and
Maria Lucas, to dine at Rosings, calling it an unheard-of mark of condescension.[^s8] On hearing of
Lydia Bennet's elopement, he writes Mr. Bennet a letter of condolence that calls it an affliction
worse than death, blames Lydia's own "naturally bad" disposition, and — with evident relief —
reflects that Elizabeth's earlier refusal of his own proposal now spares him a connection to the
family's disgrace.[^s9]

Learning through the gossiping Lucases of a rumour that Elizabeth may soon marry Darcy, Collins
writes Mr. Bennet a letter of congratulation and warning: he cautions against "a precipitate closure
with this gentleman's proposals," reporting that Lady Catherine, on hearing of the match, has said
she would never give her consent to "so disgraceful" a marriage.[^s10] The same letter renews his
disapproval of Lydia's reception at Longbourn after her elopement, urging that the Bennets ought to
"forgive them as a Christian" but never admit the couple into their sight.[^s11] When the Collinses
learn Lady Catherine is "exceedingly angry" at the engagement's confirmation, they remove to Lucas
Lodge until her temper cools.[^s12]
