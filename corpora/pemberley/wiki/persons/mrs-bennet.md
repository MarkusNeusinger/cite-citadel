Mrs. Bennet is the narrator's introduction as "a woman of mean understanding, little information,
and uncertain temper," whose life's business is to get her daughters married.[^s1] Her father was
an attorney in Meryton who left her four thousand pounds; her sister married Mr. Philips, her
brother is a tradesman settled in London.[^s2] She and [Mr. Bennet](mr-bennet.md) have five
daughters — Jane, Elizabeth, Mary, Catherine, and Lydia — of whom she considers Jane the beauty and
openly ranks [Elizabeth Bennet](elizabeth-bennet.md) below her other children.[^s3]

She treats the entail of [Longbourn](../objects/longbourn.md) away from her daughters as a
standing grievance.[^s4] When Elizabeth refuses [William Collins](william-collins.md)'s proposal,
Mrs. Bennet declares she will never speak to her again, though she does not keep to it.[^s5]

On learning of Lydia's elopement, Mrs. Bennet collapses into hysterics and keeps her room, blaming
the Forsters rather than her own indulgence, and fears her husband will be killed fighting Wickham
in a duel.[^s6] When Wickham's marriage to Lydia is confirmed, her distress turns instantly to
unclouded joy; she busies herself planning wedding clothes and searching the neighbourhood for a
house near Longbourn for the couple, undeterred by her husband's flat refusal to receive them
there.[^s7]

Lydia's departure for Newcastle leaves Mrs. Bennet "very dull for several days," lamenting the
separation though she denies it has anything to do with marriage itself.[^s8] News of Bingley's
return to Netherfield revives her spirits entirely; on seeing Darcy arrive with him she declares she
"hate[s] the very sight of him," and her open rudeness toward the man Elizabeth's family is secretly
indebted to distresses her daughters through the visit.[^s9] When Bingley proposes to Jane, her joy
is boundless, and she talks to Bingley of nothing else for half an hour.[^s10]

On learning of Elizabeth's engagement to Darcy, she is at first struck entirely speechless before
recovering to marvel repeatedly at his wealth, apologize for her past dislike of him, and worry over
what dish he prefers.[^s11] Afterward she stands in such awe of her future son-in-law that she
scarcely dares address him except with careful deference.[^s12]
