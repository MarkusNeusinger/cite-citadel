George Saintsbury wrote the preface to this 1894 edition of
[Pride and Prejudice](../objects/pride-and-prejudice.md), signed at its close.[^s1] His preface is
a personal critical argument for the novel, not a neutral summary, and its judgments below are his
own opinions rather than settled facts.

Saintsbury declares for _Pride and Prejudice_ "unhesitatingly" as [Jane Austen](jane-austen.md)'s
most perfect and characteristic work, against the more commonly favoured _Emma_.[^s2] He judges the
book's plot construction superior to Austen's other novels.[^s3] He calls the character of Mr.
Collins "really great," ranking him above anything in Addison and comparing him to Fielding or
Swift, and considers John Dashwood (in _Sense and Sensibility_) an earlier, unsuccessful sketch of
the same type.[^s4] He defends Mr. Bennet against charges of impropriety for his treatment of his
wife, calling her "a quite irreclaimable fool" and Mr. Bennet's irony the only sane response.[^s5]

He rates Darcy "by far the best and most interesting" of Austen's heroes, with Henry Tilney (of
_Northanger Abbey_) as his only rival, and singles out the change in Darcy's manner at his sudden
meeting with Elizabeth in the grounds of Pemberley as one of the finest touches in Austen's work.[^s6]
He places Elizabeth Bennet among only five heroines in a century of novels whom "no man of taste and
spirit" could help falling in love with, alongside Diana Vernon, Argemone Lavington, Beatrix Esmond,
and Barbara Grant.[^s7]
