Lydia Bennet is the youngest of the five Bennet daughters, "a stout, well-grown girl of fifteen,
with a fine complexion and good-humoured countenance," and her mother's favourite, brought into
public society at an early age.[^s1] She declares herself unafraid of being overlooked at dances
because, though youngest, she is "the tallest."[^s2] She and [Catherine Bennet](catherine-bennet.md)
walk to [Meryton](../misc/meryton.md) frequently to hear news of the officers from their aunt, Mrs.
Philips.[^s3]

She abruptly reminds [Charles Bingley](charles-bingley.md) of his promise to hold a ball at
Netherfield.[^s4] She is fond of the card game lottery tickets.[^s5] It is Lydia who, unable to
contain the news, blurts out to Sir William Lucas's household that
[William Collins](william-collins.md) wanted to marry Elizabeth, before Charlotte's own engagement
to him can be announced.[^s6]

That summer Lydia receives an invitation from [Mrs. Forster](mrs-forster.md), wife of the militia
colonel and a new, close friend of only a few months' standing, to accompany her to Brighton when
the regiment removes there; her rapture is complete, and Elizabeth's private attempt to dissuade
their father fails.[^s7] Before leaving Meryton she boasts to her sisters of dressing a fellow
officer, Chamberlayne, in women's clothes as a joke at Colonel Forster's house.[^s8]

In July, Lydia leaves Brighton with [George Wickham](george-wickham.md); her note to Mrs. Forster
declares she is going to Gretna Green with the only man she loves and jokes about signing her future
letters "Lydia Wickham," asking that her clothes be sent on and apologizing to a Mr. Pratt for
missing a dance.[^s9] The couple are found in London, not married; Wickham agrees to marry her only
once [Mr. Gardiner](mr-gardiner.md) secures a financial settlement.[^s10] Married from the
Gardiners' house, Lydia returns to Longbourn entirely unabashed, takes precedence over Jane at table
as a married woman, and shows off her ring to the servants and a passing acquaintance before the
couple depart for Wickham's new posting in the north.[^s11]

Describing her wedding to Elizabeth, Lydia relates that she married at St. Clement's, in Wickham's
own parish, escorted by the Gardiners; in the telling she carelessly lets slip that Mr. Darcy was to
have attended with Wickham, then catches herself and swears her sisters to secrecy.[^s12]

By the novel's close, marriage brings no change to Lydia's character: Wickham's affection for her
sinks into indifference sooner than hers for him, and the couple move constantly from place to
place, always spending beyond their means.[^s13] Her congratulatory letter to Elizabeth on the latter's engagement to Darcy
hopes aloud that, now Elizabeth is rich, Wickham might be found "a place at court," worth three or
four hundred a year — a request Elizabeth declines to pass on.[^s14]
