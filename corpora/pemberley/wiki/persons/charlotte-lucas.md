Charlotte Lucas is the eldest daughter of [Sir William Lucas](william-lucas.md) and
[Elizabeth Bennet](elizabeth-bennet.md)'s intimate friend, "a sensible, intelligent young woman,
about twenty-seven."[^s1] She argues that "happiness in marriage is entirely a matter of chance"
and that it is better to know as little as possible of a future spouse's defects — a view Elizabeth
finds unsound.[^s2]

Days after Elizabeth refuses [William Collins](william-collins.md), Charlotte secures his proposal
for herself, reasoning that marriage is "the only honourable provision for well-educated young
women of small fortune" and that she is not romantic.[^s3] Her father and mother consent with
"joyful alacrity."[^s4] The engagement strains her friendship with Elizabeth, who is privately
appalled that Charlotte "sacrificed every better feeling to worldly advantage."[^s5] Charlotte
marries Collins and departs for Kent; before leaving she extracts a promise from Elizabeth to visit
her at Hunsford.[^s6]

At Hunsford, Charlotte manages her household and her husband's absurdities with practiced composure,
and privately arranges the sitting rooms so that Collins spends most of his day away from the
family, in his own book-room.[^s7] Watching Darcy's repeated, silent visits to the parsonage, she
suspects he is in love with Elizabeth, though Elizabeth laughs off the idea.[^s8]

On hearing of Elizabeth's engagement to Darcy, Charlotte — "really rejoicing in the match" — brings
Collins away to stay at Lucas Lodge until Lady Catherine's anger over it has "blown over."[^s9]
