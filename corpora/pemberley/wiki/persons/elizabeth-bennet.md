Elizabeth Bennet is the second daughter of [Mr.](mr-bennet.md) and [Mrs. Bennet](mrs-bennet.md),
called "Lizzy" by her family and "Eliza" by [Charlotte Lucas](charlotte-lucas.md).[^s1] Her father
considers her to have "something more of quickness than her sisters," and the narrator describes
her as having "a lively, playful disposition, which delighted in anything ridiculous."[^s2] At the
Meryton assembly, [Fitzwilliam Darcy](fitzwilliam-darcy.md) first dismisses her as "tolerable, but
not handsome enough to tempt _me_"; over the following weeks at Netherfield he comes to admire her
eyes, figure, and manner instead.[^s3]

When [Jane Bennet](jane-bennet.md) falls ill at [Netherfield Park](../objects/netherfield-park.md),
Elizabeth walks three miles alone through the mud to nurse her.[^s4] [William Collins](william-collins.md)
proposes marriage to her; she refuses him firmly and repeatedly despite her mother's demands that
she accept.[^s5] She dances with Darcy at the Netherfield ball, sparring with him over his temper
and his unforgiving nature.[^s6]

Elizabeth is drawn to the newly arrived officer [George Wickham](george-wickham.md) and readily
believes his account that Darcy denied him a living his father had promised him.[^s7] Her friendship
with Charlotte Lucas is strained after Charlotte accepts Collins's proposal in Elizabeth's place.[^s8]
Her aunt, [Mrs. Gardiner](mrs-gardiner.md), cautions her against encouraging Wickham given his want
of fortune; Elizabeth promises to be prudent.[^s9]

In March, Elizabeth travels to Hunsford, Kent, to visit Charlotte, accompanying Sir William and
Maria Lucas; she parts from Wickham on easy, friendly terms before leaving.[^s10] Dining repeatedly
at [Rosings Park](../objects/rosings-park.md), she endures Lady Catherine's intrusive questioning
about her family, her age, and her upbringing, answering with composed wit rather than
embarrassment.[^s11] [Colonel Fitzwilliam](colonel-fitzwilliam.md), visiting his aunt with Darcy,
pays her marked attention, while Darcy himself calls at the parsonage unannounced and, in a
strained conversation, questions her about her attachment to Hertfordshire.[^s12] When Fitzwilliam
unknowingly reveals that Darcy boasted of recently saving a friend from an "imprudent marriage,"
Elizabeth deduces he means Bingley and Jane, and is deeply angered.[^s13] Darcy then proposes
marriage to her at the parsonage; she refuses him unequivocally, charging him with ruining Jane's
happiness and with injuring Wickham.[^s14]

The next morning Darcy hands her a letter defending both actions: he believed Jane indifferent to
Bingley and objected to the impropriety of her family, and he sets out his own account of Wickham's
history. Reading and re-reading it, Elizabeth's convictions reverse; she comes to blame her own
vanity and lack of judgment, exclaiming to herself, "How despicably have I acted!"[^s15]

Back at Longbourn she confides Darcy's proposal to Jane, then the substance of his letter regarding
Wickham, but decides against exposing Wickham publicly since the regiment is soon to leave Meryton,
and withholds the part of the letter concerning Georgiana Darcy.[^s16] She privately urges her
father not to let Lydia accept Mrs. Forster's invitation to Brighton, without success, and at her
last meeting with Wickham before the regiment departs, hints that she now knows the truth about him
— unsettling him without naming her source.[^s17]

On a tour of Derbyshire with the Gardiners that summer, she visits [Pemberley](../objects/pemberley.md)
believing Darcy from home; the housekeeper [Mrs. Reynolds](mrs-reynolds.md)'s extensive praise of
his character softens her opinion of him considerably.[^s18] An unexpected, strikingly civil
encounter with Darcy himself follows, including an introduction to his sister and an invitation for
the Gardiners and Elizabeth to dine at Pemberley.[^s19] At Lambton she receives Jane's letters
revealing that Lydia has eloped with Wickham; distraught, she tells Darcy the whole situation when
he calls, breaking her resolve to keep it private, and hurries home with the Gardiners.[^s20] She
comes to regret confiding in Darcy, believing the disgrace will end any hope of his renewed interest
— which she now recognizes she wants — and later suspects her uncle paid heavily to secure Wickham
and Lydia's marriage.[^s21]

Back at Longbourn, Lydia carelessly reveals that Mr. Darcy attended her wedding — a secret she was
not meant to disclose — throwing Elizabeth into astonishment; unable to bear the suspense, she
writes at once to Mrs. Gardiner for an explanation.[^s22] Mrs. Gardiner's reply reveals that Darcy
found Wickham and Lydia in London and personally arranged and paid for their marriage, swearing her
uncle to secrecy about it.[^s23] The revelation throws Elizabeth into a flutter of mixed pleasure and
pain; ashamed of every ungracious word she ever directed at him, she nonetheless doubts his motive
could be renewed affection for a woman who had already refused him.[^s24]

Overtaken by Wickham on a solitary walk, she confirms she has seen Pemberley and spoken with Mrs.
Reynolds, and, when he probes about Darcy's business in London, suggests without revealing what she
knows that he may be preparing to marry Miss de Bourgh.[^s25]

When Bingley returns to Netherfield bringing Darcy with him, Elizabeth is distressed by her mother's
rudeness to the man her family owes everything to, and by Darcy's grave, reserved manner in
company, so unlike his conduct at Pemberley.[^s26] At a dinner at Longbourn some days later she
exchanges only a few words with him, learning that Georgiana will remain at Pemberley with her
companion Mrs. Annesley until Christmas.[^s27]

Lady Catherine de Bourgh arrives at Longbourn unannounced and, walking with Elizabeth in the
grounds, demands she deny a rumoured engagement to Darcy; Elizabeth declines to answer many of her
questions and refuses to promise never to accept him.[^s28] She coolly observes that the wife of Mr.
Darcy would have such extraordinary sources of happiness that she could have no cause to
repine.[^s29] Pressed by her mother afterward, she conceals the true subject of the visit.[^s30]

Mr. Bennet reads aloud Mr. Collins's letter warning that Lady Catherine disapproves of the rumoured
Darcy match; forced to laugh along at her father's jest that Darcy "never looks at any woman but to
see a blemish," Elizabeth can manage only a reluctant smile and later wonders whether he has, in
fact, seen too little.[^s31]

When Darcy returns to Longbourn with Bingley, Elizabeth walks out alone with him and thanks him for
his kindness to Lydia; he declares his affections and wishes unchanged, and she tells him her own
sentiments have so materially changed that she now receives his renewed address with gratitude and
pleasure — they become engaged.[^s32] She learns that Lady Catherine's visit, meant to end any hope
of the match, had instead given Darcy fresh reason to hope, since her defiant answers convinced him
she was not irrevocably against him.[^s33] Reflecting together on their bitter exchange at Hunsford,
she admits she has long been "heartily ashamed" of what she said there.[^s34]

That night she tells Jane, who at first refuses to believe it and is only convinced by repeated,
solemn assurance; Elizabeth confesses she dates her attachment "from my first seeing his beautiful
grounds at Pemberley."[^s35] The next morning she wins her father's consent, overcoming his disbelief
that she — who he thought had "always hated" Darcy — could really love him, by recounting the
gradual change in her opinion; hearing afterward what Darcy did for Lydia, Mr. Bennet declares Darcy
"deserves" her.[^s36] Her mother, struck speechless at first, soon recovers to marvel at Darcy's ten
thousand a year.[^s37]

Elizabeth writes a teasing letter to Mrs. Gardiner announcing the engagement, thanking her for not
going to the Lakes and inviting the Gardiners to Pemberley at Christmas.[^s38] After her marriage she
persuades Darcy to seek a reconciliation with Lady Catherine, and remains on the most intimate terms
with the Gardiners.[^s39]
