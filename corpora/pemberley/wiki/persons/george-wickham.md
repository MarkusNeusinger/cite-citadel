George Wickham is introduced to the Bennet family in the street at [Meryton](../misc/meryton.md)
by his friend Mr. Denny, having just accepted a lieutenant's commission in the militia corps
stationed there.[^s1] He is immediately popular: "he had all the best parts of beauty, a fine
countenance, a good figure, and very pleasing address."[^s2]

His father managed the [Pemberley](../objects/pemberley.md) property as steward to the late Mr.
Darcy; Wickham was born in the same parish and grew up within the same park as
[Fitzwilliam Darcy](fitzwilliam-darcy.md), sharing his amusements and upbringing.[^s3] By his own
account to [Elizabeth Bennet](elizabeth-bennet.md), the late Mr. Darcy was his godfather and had
promised him the next presentation of a valuable living, intending to provide for him amply; when
the living fell vacant, however, the present Mr. Darcy gave it to another man, treating the
bequest as merely conditional. Wickham says he was consequently trained for the church but is now
in the militia instead — an account this segment does not independently confirm.[^s4]
[Caroline Bingley](caroline-bingley.md) separately tells Elizabeth that Darcy has always treated
Wickham kindly and that Wickham has treated Darcy "in a most infamous manner," directly
contradicting Wickham's own version.[^s5]

He is absent from the Netherfield ball, which he tells Elizabeth afterward was to avoid meeting
Darcy there.[^s6] By the end of this segment, his attentions have shifted from Elizabeth to a Miss
King, who has just inherited ten thousand pounds.[^s7]

> [!CONTRADICTION]
> Wickham told Elizabeth that the present Mr. Darcy withheld a living the late Mr. Darcy had
> promised him, treating a bequest he describes as unconditional.[^s4] Darcy's own letter to
> Elizabeth states instead that Wickham voluntarily resigned all claim to the living for three
> thousand pounds, and only demanded it again years later, after squandering the money and living
> idly.[^s8]
>
> Resolution: Darcy's account is corroborated within the letter itself by Colonel Fitzwilliam, a
> co-executor of the late Mr. Darcy's will, and independently by Mrs. Reynolds's testimony and the
> Lambton townspeople's knowledge of debts Wickham left behind in Derbyshire; Elizabeth herself
> comes to believe Darcy's version entirely.[^llm1]

Darcy's letter further states that last summer Wickham attempted to elope with the
then-fifteen-year-old [Georgiana Darcy](georgiana-darcy.md) at Ramsgate, aided by her companion Mrs.
Younge, chiefly for her thirty-thousand-pound fortune but also, Darcy believes, from a wish for
revenge; the scheme collapsed when Georgiana confessed it to her brother.[^s9]

Before Elizabeth's departure for Kent that spring, Wickham parts from her on friendly terms.[^s10]
By then his pursuit of Miss King has come to nothing — she has gone to her uncle in Liverpool — and
Elizabeth privately reflects that his conduct differs little from what she once excused as
prudence.[^s11] At their last meeting before the regiment leaves Meryton for Brighton, Wickham
renews his attentions to her; she deflects him but hints she now knows the truth about him, and he
is visibly alarmed without learning her source.[^s12]

In July he and [Lydia Bennet](lydia-bennet.md) elope together from Brighton, leaving a note to Mrs.
Forster that names Gretna Green as their destination, though Mr. Denny doubts he ever meant to go
there or to marry her at all; Colonel Forster traces them only as far as Clapham, and it emerges
that Wickham has no near relations living and left considerable gaming debts behind him, reckoned at
well over a thousand pounds.[^s13] [Mr. Gardiner](mr-gardiner.md) finds the pair in London and
secures Wickham's agreement to marry Lydia in exchange for a settlement of her share of the Bennet
family's five thousand pounds and one hundred pounds a year during Mr. Bennet's life.[^s14] Wickham
resolves to quit the militia for an ensigncy in the Regulars, in a regiment quartered in the north;
married from the Gardiners' house, the couple visit Longbourn for ten days — Wickham as much at ease
as ever — before departing for Newcastle.[^s15]

Per Mrs. Gardiner's letter, Darcy tracked Wickham down in London through [Mrs. Younge](mrs-younge.md),
a former companion of Georgiana's with whom Wickham was intimately acquainted; Wickham had never
intended to marry Lydia, and his consent was secured only once his mounting debts left him "reduced
to be reasonable."[^s16] He resigns his militia commission on account of debts of honour and, with
Darcy's money, purchases an ensigncy in the Regulars instead.[^s17] After the wedding he calls Lydia
"my dear sister" and, in a walk with Elizabeth, questions her closely about her visit to Pemberley,
Mrs. Reynolds, Georgiana, and the Kympton living he still believes he was cheated of, before
Elizabeth's pointed answers silence him.[^s18] His adieus on leaving Longbourn for Newcastle are
noted as "much more affectionate than his wife's."[^s19]

By the novel's close, his affection for Lydia has sunk into indifference; the couple move
constantly from place to place, always spending beyond their income, and rely on occasional help
from Jane and Elizabeth to cover their bills.[^s20] Though Darcy can never receive him at Pemberley,
he continues, for Elizabeth's sake, to assist Wickham in his profession; Wickham, for his part, is
not wholly without hope that Darcy might yet help make his fortune.[^s21]
