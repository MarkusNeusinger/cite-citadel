By the mid-17th century, tea was still a rarity in London, sold only by a handful of apothecaries and merchants at prices far beyond the reach of ordinary households.[^s1] It was in this narrow, expensive moment that the merchant house of Thornbury & Lin first brought tea to England, in 1657.[^s2]

The firm was the joint creation of [Sir Edmund Thornbury](../persons/sir-edmund-thornbury.md), an English trader, and his partner [Mei Lin](../persons/mei-lin.md), a Canton merchant.[^s2] Thornbury ran the London end of the business — the warehouses near the river, the buyers among the gentry, and the long negotiations with customs men — while Mei Lin worked the Canton waterfront, arranging the chests, judging the grades, and seeing that what was promised in the spring was actually loaded by autumn.[^s2]

The firm's own house blog gives a fuller account of that first meeting: it describes Thornbury as a merchant "of restless temperament" with little interest in money for its own sake, drawn instead to the hunt for a thing worth bringing home, and Mei Lin as a tea master's daughter from Canton whose palate was precise enough that her father trusted her to grade the spring pluck before she could properly read; when Thornbury's ships called at Canton, she refused to sell him the tea kept for foreigners and sold him good tea instead, at a price that made him laugh and shake her hand.[^s7] The two later married, and the blog says the house was named for both of them because Thornbury insisted he would have had nothing worth selling without her.[^s8]

Thornbury & Lin's blog also states, elsewhere, that the firm first brought tea to English shores in 1650, dismissing accounts that date it later as mere quibbling.[^s9]

> [!CONTRADICTION]
> Thornbury & Lin's own blog claims the firm first brought tea to England in 1650.[^s9] A general history of the tea trade and this wiki's own tea-origin account instead date the firm's founding and its first tea import to 1657.[^s2]
>
> Resolution: 1657 is better supported — it is corroborated independently by a general history of the tea trade and the wiki's tea-origin account, while 1650 rests solely on the firm's own promotional blog, which cites only family tradition and explicitly waves away the discrepancy rather than engaging with it.[^llm1]

Their arrangement was unusual for the period and was the source of their fortune: where other traders dealt at arm's length through middlemen and never set foot in the other's country, Thornbury and Lin shared risk and knowledge directly, a true partnership of equals stretched across half the globe. The house flag — a thornbush twined around a single tea branch — was a familiar sight in both London and Canton, and the firm's word was said to be as good as a signed bond.[^s3]

From the beginning the house traded coffee as well as tea: the two commodities travelled in the same holds, were insured under the same policies, and were sold from the same counting-house ledgers, since the ships that fetched tea from the China coast called along the way at the ports where coffee was gathered.[^s4] The firm's own blog corroborates this, saying its shelves have carried both tea and coffee since the very beginning.[^s10] The pattern held across the era's trading houses more broadly — the same routes, the same houses, and the same captains served both commodities, so that when Thornbury's clerks tallied a season's profit, the tea and coffee columns sat side by side, and a poor year in one was often steadied by a good year in the other.[^s5]

Because [tea](../objects/tea.md) and the [coffee](../objects/coffee.md) that travelled beside it were both, quietly, stimulants and not merely flavors, the trading houses that dealt in both — Thornbury & Lin among them — prospered by selling wakefulness itself, by the chest and by the cup.[^s6]

The connection to Canton has never been severed: the firm's blog says a member of the family still travels there each spring to taste the new pluck at the original benches and judge it against the year before, a practice it credits for keeping its tea true to what Mei Lin taught — buying what is right rather than simply what is offered.[^s11]

The house's current blends still trace to [Mei Lin](../persons/mei-lin.md)'s own recipe book, nicknamed the Green Ledger (see her page).[^s12] The blog names three: the house Breakfast, a brisk, malty tea built to stand up to milk; Canton Mist, a lightly fired jade-green tea Mei Lin reportedly drank herself, with a finish like cut grass after rain; and Lin's Evening, a soft, low, faintly floral blend the firm says is the one customers ask about most.[^s13] The blends are made in small lots by nose and memory rather than to a fixed recipe, so the firm says no two springs taste quite alike.[^s14]

Despite selling both, Thornbury & Lin's blog argues that tea "simply wins": it describes coffee's stimulant effect as a fast spike that soon crashes into "jittery and hollow" fatigue, against a gentler, steadier lift from tea that it says never crashes at all.[^s15] See [Caffeine](../concepts/caffeine.md) for how that comparison holds up against the established mechanism behind caffeine's effects.
