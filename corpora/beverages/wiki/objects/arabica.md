Arabica, botanically *Coffea arabica*, is one of the two species — alongside [Robusta](robusta.md) — that account for essentially all commercial [coffee](coffee.md) production.[^s1] It is the dominant species in the global market, supplying roughly 60 to 70 percent of world production.[^s2] Arabica is generally grown at higher altitudes, where cooler temperatures slow the maturation of the cherry and allow more complex sugars and aromatic compounds to develop; the result is a cup typically regarded as sweeter, more nuanced, and more layered in flavor, often carrying notes described as floral, fruity, or chocolate-like.[^s2] Arabica plants are, however, comparatively delicate: they are sensitive to temperature swings, vulnerable to pests, and especially susceptible to coffee leaf rust, a fungal disease that has historically devastated entire growing regions.[^s2]

See [Coffee](coffee.md) for a side-by-side comparison of Arabica's and Robusta's traits.
