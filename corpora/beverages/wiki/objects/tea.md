Tea is one of the most widely consumed beverages on the planet, second only to water in the volume drunk each day.[^s1] Despite the enormous variety of products sold under the name, the word "tea" in its strict sense refers to an infusion prepared from the leaves of a single plant species, [*Camellia sinensis*](camellia-sinensis.md).[^s1]

All true tea comes from that one plant. Beverages brewed from other plants — rooibos, chamomile, peppermint, hibiscus, or lemon balm — are properly called herbal infusions or tisanes; they are not tea in the botanical sense, however much they may resemble it in the cup.[^s2][^s6]

What distinguishes white, green, oolong, black, and pu-erh tea from one another is not the species of plant but the way the harvested leaf is treated afterward — chiefly the degree of oxidation it undergoes before that reaction is halted by heat. See [Tea processing](../concepts/tea-processing.md) for how the same fresh leaf becomes each of these radically different products, including a rarer sixth class, yellow tea, and the distinction between fully-oxidised black tea and the post-fermented dark tea class that pu-erh belongs to.[^s3]

## Comparison of the major types

The table below summarizes the defining attributes of the principal tea families, including their place on the oxidation scale, recommended brewing temperature, and the approximate caffeine content of a standard cup.[^s4]

| Type    | Oxidation        | Brew Temp (°C) | Caffeine (per cup) |
|---------|------------------|----------------|--------------------|
| White   | Very little      | 75–85          | ~30–55 mg          |
| Green   | Little           | 70–80          | ~28 mg             |
| Oolong  | Partial          | 85–95          | ~37 mg             |
| Black   | Full             | 95–100         | ~47 mg             |
| Pu-erh  | Fermented / aged | 95–100         | ~40 mg             |

See [Tea processing](../concepts/tea-processing.md) for the oxidation spectrum behind this table, [Tea brewing](../concepts/tea-brewing.md) for brewing guidance, and [Caffeine](../concepts/caffeine.md) for more on tea's caffeine content.

## Health

Tea is mostly water and, unsweetened, naturally low in calories; the leaf's handful of active compounds — chiefly caffeine, antioxidant catechins, and the calming amino acid [L-theanine](../concepts/l-theanine.md) — are what give tea most of its reputation as a healthful drink.[^s7] Despite an old myth that tea dehydrates the drinker, a normal cup is mostly water and contributes to daily hydration; its caffeine levels are far too low to have any meaningful dehydrating effect.[^s8]

Sweetening changes the calorie math: the tea itself is fine, but a heavily sweetened or sugary bottled tea can carry a substantial amount of added sugar and calories, so unsweetened or lightly sweetened tea is the better choice for anyone watching their intake.[^s9]

## Storage

Tea is best kept away from its four great enemies: light, heat, moisture, and strong odours. Most green and black teas are at their best within a year or two of production and should be stored in an opaque, airtight container in a cool, dry place. [Pu-erh](pu-erh.md) is a notable exception: like wine, it is often deliberately aged for years or decades, and a well-stored cake may grow more valued over time.[^s5]
