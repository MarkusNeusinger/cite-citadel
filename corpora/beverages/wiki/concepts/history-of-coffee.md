[Coffee](../objects/coffee.md) was first cultivated in the highlands of Ethiopia, where *Coffea arabica* grows wild and where the practice of preparing the seeds as a drink is believed to have originated.[^s1] From this East African cradle, coffee crossed the Red Sea and took root in Yemen, where it was systematically cultivated and traded; the Yemeni port of Mocha became so closely associated with the commodity that its name endures as a coffee term to this day.[^s1]

Through Yemen and the wider Arab world, coffee spread as both a commercial good and a social institution: coffeehouses became centers of conversation, commerce, and learning across the region long before the drink was known in Europe.[^s2] By the 17th century coffee had reached the European continent, where it was initially regarded as an exotic novelty before rapidly becoming a fixture of urban life, and European coffeehouses grew into gathering places for merchants, writers, and political thinkers, much as their Arab predecessors had.[^s2]

As demand grew, colonial powers carried coffee cultivation to tropical regions across the globe, establishing plantations throughout Asia, Africa, and the Americas, which fundamentally reshaped the geography of production.[^s3] Today the largest producer of coffee in the world is Brazil, whose vast plantations supply an enormous share of the global market; Vietnam ranks second, having risen to prominence largely on the strength of [Robusta](../objects/robusta.md) cultivation.[^s3] Other significant producers include Colombia, Indonesia, Ethiopia, and several nations of Central America.[^s3]

Coffee's early European trade followed much the same routes as tea's. The 17th-century merchant house [Thornbury & Lin](../organizations/thornbury-lin.md), best known for first bringing tea to England in 1657, traded coffee alongside it from the outset: the two commodities travelled in the same holds, were insured under the same policies, and were sold from the same ledgers, since ships that fetched tea from the China coast called at the same ports where coffee was gathered.[^s4] The pattern held across the era's trading houses more broadly — the same routes, houses, and captains served both commodities, so that coffee and tea arrived in Europe and rose in fortune almost as twins.[^s5]
