Caffeine produces its stimulant effect by blocking adenosine receptors in the brain. Adenosine is the molecule that builds up over the course of the day and creates "sleep pressure" — the drowsy feeling that grows the longer a person has been awake — and caffeine's shape is similar enough to occupy the same receptors without activating them, so the brain stops receiving the signal to wind down and the drinker feels alert; caffeine does not add energy, it masks a tiredness that keeps accumulating underneath.[^s8] The adenosine itself never stops building while caffeine is active in the body — caffeine only blocks the receptors, it does not remove the molecule — so once the caffeine wears off, the whole backlog of adenosine reaches the receptors at once, one explanation for a sudden afternoon energy slump.[^s9] Caffeine's half-life, the time for half of it to clear the bloodstream, is about five hours, so a strong afternoon coffee can still leave a noticeable amount of caffeine in the body at bedtime even after the wired feeling has faded.[^s10] That figure varies from person to person — genetics, liver enzymes, smoking, certain medications, and pregnancy can all lengthen or shorten it, so "about five hours" is a solid average for a healthy adult rather than a fixed number, and someone who reliably struggles to sleep after coffee may simply metabolize caffeine more slowly than average.[^s11] Because of that roughly five-hour half-life, a common rule of thumb is to have the day's last full-strength coffee at least six to eight hours before bedtime — earlier still for a slow metabolizer, or swapped for decaf in the evening.[^s12]

> [!CONTRADICTION]
> Coffee-health guidance gives caffeine's half-life as about 5 hours on average.[^s10] Cordwell Roastworks' cold brew bench notes, prepared for staff-facing menu cards, put it at about 3 hours.[^s23]

For most healthy adults, a few cups of coffee spread across the day is generally considered fine, and tolerance tends to drift higher the more regularly a person drinks coffee; problems usually arise when caffeine piles up quickly, with jitteriness, a racing heart, anxiety, and trouble sleeping as the common warning signs, calling for a few caffeine-light days to ease off.[^s14] That tolerance has a mechanism: with regular intake the body grows more adenosine receptors, so the same dose has less effect over time and a habitual drinker needs somewhat more caffeine for the same lift; the flip side is dependence, and skipping a usual coffee can bring on a withdrawal headache, fatigue, or irritability for a day or two before it passes — tapering down slowly is markedly easier than quitting outright.[^s15]

During pregnancy, common guidance is to keep caffeine under about 200 mg a day — roughly two standard 8 oz cups of drip coffee — while accounting for the caffeine in tea, chocolate, soft drinks, and energy drinks, since it all adds together; a pregnant person's own doctor's advice comes first.[^s16] Caffeine also turns up outside coffee: chocolate, many teas, some sodas, energy drinks, and even certain pain relievers and supplements contain it, and all of it counts toward a daily limit the same as coffee does.[^s17] Decaf coffee keeps most of the flavor and ritual of regular coffee while removing the large majority of its caffeine, making it a reasonable choice later in the day or for cutting back — but "decaffeinated" does not mean caffeine-free, and a trace remains.[^s18]

Large studies that follow groups of people over time have found that drinking 3-4 cups of coffee a day is associated with a lower risk of type 2 diabetes and Parkinson's disease; the finding is observational, so it shows a pattern across populations rather than proof that coffee itself prevents either condition — people who drink moderate coffee may differ from others in ways that also affect their risk, and association is not the same as cause.[^s19] Showing that coffee directly causes the lower risk would need controlled trials, which are hard to run over the decades such conditions take to develop; because association studies cannot separate coffee from everything else about a person's life, the fair summary is that moderate coffee drinking is linked to some good outcomes, with a signal consistent enough to be worth attention, but "linked to" is as far as the evidence goes — coffee should not be taken up purely as medicine.[^s20]

Two common coffee claims do not hold up. Caffeine is a mild diuretic and can prompt a bathroom trip a little sooner, but the water in a cup of coffee more than offsets that, so for most people normal coffee drinking still contributes to daily fluid intake rather than causing dehydration.[^s21] And the idea that coffee stunts growth is an old myth with no good evidence behind it — caffeine does not shrink anyone; the real, narrower concern for children and teens is that caffeine late in the day can disrupt the sleep that growing bodies need, which is a matter of sleep, not height.[^s22]

[Caffè Aurora](../organizations/caffe-aurora.md)'s spring 2024 bulletin claims that coffee begins losing its caffeine the moment it leaves the roaster: it says fresh-roasted beans carry their peak flavor and kick on the day they tumble out of the drum, with the caffeine then quietly fading week by week alongside the aroma as the bag sits on a shelf, so that a month-old bag does not wake a drinker up the way a fresh one does.[^s1] The roastery gives this as its reason for roasting in small batches, dating every bag, and preferring to sell a small bag often rather than a big bag once.[^s1]

This conflicts with the established understanding that caffeine is a chemically stable compound that does not measurably degrade at room temperature over the weeks a roasted bag sits on a shelf — what actually fades with age is the coffee's volatile aroma and flavor compounds, through staling and oxidation, not its caffeine content.[^llm1]

Caffè Aurora's spring 2026 bulletin adds a second caffeine claim: it says cold brew is the lowest-caffeine drink on its menu, reasoning that brewing cold pulls out far less caffeine than hot water ever could, and recommends it to customers who want the flavor without the buzz.[^s2] This conflicts with the common understanding among coffee educators that cold brew's much longer steep time and typically higher coffee-to-water ratio tend to give it caffeine levels comparable to or higher than hot-brewed coffee, not lower — actual caffeine content depends heavily on the ratio and steep time used, not the water temperature alone.[^llm2] [Cordwell Roastworks' own cold brew R&D](cold-brew.md) bears this out: at its house 1:8 concentrate ratio with a 12-18 hour steep, the roastery's bench notes put a cold brew concentrate at roughly 2.5x the caffeine of a standard hot drip mug before dilution, attributing the gap to the long contact time and heavy grounds-to-water ratio rather than to the water being cold.[^s24]

Brewing method does change how much caffeine ends up in the cup, though not through concentration alone: a single espresso shot is far more concentrated than drip coffee — roughly 8-12% [TDS](../abbreviations/tds-total-dissolved-solids.md) against drip's 1.15-1.35% — yet because the shot itself is so small, the total caffeine in one espresso (about 63 mg)[^s25] is actually less than in a full mug of drip (about 95 mg); concentration and total dose are different measures, the same distinction the cold-brew claim above elides.[^s4][^s5][^s13] That 95 mg figure is itself a baseline for an 8 oz cup — a real-world cup is often bigger, and the caffeine scales up with it.[^s13]

[Tea](../objects/tea.md) is coffee's most common caffeine comparison. Like coffee, tea naturally contains caffeine, and the amount in a finished cup depends on the type of tea, the quantity of leaf used, the water temperature, and the length of the steep.[^s27] A cup of black tea contains about 47 mg of caffeine, while a cup of green tea contains about 28 mg; the lower figure for green tea reflects both its cooler brewing water and its shorter typical steep rather than any fundamental shortage of caffeine in the leaf itself.[^s28] As a rough rule of thumb, a cup of black tea carries roughly half the caffeine of a cup of drip coffee — about 47 mg against about 95 mg.[^s30] That gentler dose gives more room to enjoy several cups of tea across the day without pushing total caffeine intake too high.[^s35] White tea is a common point of confusion: despite being the least processed of the teas, it is not caffeine-free — at roughly 30 to 55 mg per cup, its very young, compound-rich buds can give it a caffeine content that rivals or exceeds that of some green and even black teas, so avoiding caffeine entirely calls for a genuine herbal infusion rather than any product of the tea plant.[^s29]

> [!CONTRADICTION]
> A history of the tea trade puts green tea at about 50 mg of caffeine per cup, "very nearly the same" as black tea.[^s36] Tea's own brewing guidance instead gives green tea about 28 mg and black tea about 47 mg per cup — a real gap, not a near-match.[^s28]
>
> Resolution: the lower, differentiated figures are better supported here — they are corroborated independently by both the tea guide's brewing chart and the tea health FAQ, and they match the well-established explanation that green tea's cooler brewing water and shorter steep, not any shortage of caffeine in the leaf, account for the gap with black tea.[^llm4]

Despite containing real caffeine, tea earns a reputation as the calmer of the two drinks because that caffeine acts alongside [L-theanine](l-theanine.md), an amino acid that smooths caffeine's sharp edge into a steadier, more focused alertness rather than a sudden jolt.[^s31] Tea's calm comes from L-theanine working alongside its caffeine, not from being caffeine-free.[^s32] As with coffee, drinking tea too close to bedtime can still disrupt sleep — the dose is gentler, but the stimulant effect is the same, so a sensitive drinker should treat an evening cup of tea with the same caution as an evening coffee.[^s33] A good general habit is to switch to caffeine-free options by the late afternoon or early evening, with an even larger buffer for anyone who metabolizes caffeine slowly.[^s34]

[Thornbury & Lin](../organizations/thornbury-lin.md)'s house blog goes further still, arguing that coffee's stimulant effect is a fast spike that inevitably "crashes" into "jittery and hollow" fatigue, while tea's lift is gentler, steadier, and never crashes at all.[^s37] This overstates the contrast: the crash it describes matches the adenosine-rebound mechanism above, which is a property of caffeine itself rather than of coffee specifically, so a strong dose of tea's own caffeine is subject to the same rebound; L-theanine changes the character of tea's caffeine hit without eliminating its stimulant effect, which is why an evening cup of tea carries the same sleep-disruption caution as an evening coffee.[^llm5]

Coffee is not the only whole-leaf-versus-infusion comparison worth making: because [matcha](../objects/matcha.md) is whisked as whole ground leaf rather than steeped and discarded, it carries more caffeine than the same weight of steeped green tea — roughly 60-70 mg per bowl, in the same range as the 63-95 mg espresso and drip figures above.[^s26]

Caffè Aurora's promotional blog goes further still, marketing [Aurora Midnight](../objects/aurora-midnight.md) — its darkest roast — as "naturally caffeine-free": it claims caffeine "burns off in the roaster," that the longer and darker a bean is taken the more caffeine the roasting fire consumes, and offers the general rule that the darker the roast, the less caffeine a coffee carries, with its blonde and medium roasts said to carry the most kick and Midnight "effectively none at all."[^s3] This conflicts with the established science of caffeine and roasting: caffeine is a thermally stable compound that survives ordinary roasting temperatures largely intact, so no roast — however dark — becomes meaningfully decaffeinated, let alone caffeine-free, without an actual decaffeination process. What changes with roast level is mainly bean density and moisture loss from longer roasting, which very slightly reduces caffeine by weight in a given volume of beans — nowhere near to zero, and a far smaller effect than the "burns off" explanation implies.[^llm3] General coffee references bear this out directly: [roast level](coffee-roasting.md) barely changes coffee's caffeine content, because caffeine is a remarkably stable molecule that does not break down meaningfully at the temperatures reached during roasting — measured by weight, light and dark roasts are nearly equal in caffeine.[^s6] The minor differences some people do observe are a matter of measurement, not caffeine being destroyed in the roaster: because dark-roasted beans lose more moisture and expand more, they are less dense, so a scoop measured by volume of dark roast contains marginally fewer beans while a portion measured by weight contains marginally more, since each bean weighs less.[^s7] The species used — [Robusta](../objects/robusta.md) carries roughly twice the caffeine of [Arabica](../objects/arabica.md) — and the overall dose of coffee relative to water influence the caffeine in the cup far more than roast level does.[^s7]
