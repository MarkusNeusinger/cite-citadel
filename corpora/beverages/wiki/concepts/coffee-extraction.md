Cordwell Roastworks hands new brewers an internal brew-lab reference sheet covering what extraction is, the numbers the [Specialty Coffee Association](../abbreviations/sca-specialty-coffee-association.md) (SCA) publishes as a target, and how grind, time, temperature, and water shape a cup.[^s1] Brewing coffee is dissolving: roasted coffee is only about 28-30% water-soluble by mass, with the rest — insoluble plant cellulose — staying behind in the grounds. Extraction yield is the fraction of the dry coffee that ends up dissolved in the cup: the dissolved solids in the drink divided by the mass of dry grounds used. Too little extraction leaves only the light, soluble, sour compounds; too much drags out the heavy, bitter, drying ones that come last.[^s2]

The compounds leave the bean in a fixed order. Acids and bright, fruity aromatics are the most soluble and come out first — an under-extracted cup tastes sour, sharp, salty, and hollow. Sugars and round, balanced flavors follow in the middle, the target window where sweetness lives. Bitter alkaloids, larger polyphenols, and dry, astringent tannins come last — an over-extracted cup tastes bitter, ashy, and drying. "Dialing for sweetness" means stopping after the sugars and before the bitterness sets in.[^s3]

The SCA "Golden Cup" target holds two numbers together. Extraction yield should land at 18-22% of the coffee's mass dissolved — below about 18% is under-extracted (sour, weak, salty), above about 22% is over-extracted (bitter, astringent, hollow), and roughly 20% is dead centre.[^s4] Strength should land at about 1.15-1.35% [TDS](../abbreviations/tds-total-dissolved-solids.md) in the finished brew by mass — roughly 11.5-13.5 grams of dissolved coffee per litre — though some references widen the pleasant band to about 1.45%.[^s5] Strength and extraction are independent axes: a brew can be strong-but-under-extracted (a lot of coffee, steeped briefly) or weak-but-over-extracted (a little coffee, steeped forever); the Golden Cup asks for both to land in their windows at once.[^s6]

Getting there starts with the ratio: the SCA "golden ratio" is about 55 g of coffee per litre of water, plus or minus 10% (roughly 50-60 g/L) — for filter, about a 1:16 to 1:18 coffee-to-water ratio by weight. More coffee per litre raises strength; grind and time then decide where extraction lands inside that strength. Target water temperature at the bed is 93 °C, plus or minus 3 (about 200 °F).[^s7] General brewing guidance states the typical ratio somewhat more broadly, at about 1:15 to 1:18 by weight.[^s20]

Extraction is surface area times solubility times time, and three levers control it. Grind size is the coarse adjustment: finer grounds expose more surface and extract faster and more, coarser grounds extract slower and less — one or two grind steps moves a cup further than anything else.[^s8] Contact time is paired to grind, method by method: espresso is very fine grounds plus about 25-30 seconds; filter and pour-over are medium grind plus about 3-4 minutes; French press is coarse grind plus about 4 minutes; [cold brew](cold-brew.md) is very coarse grind plus 12-18 hours. Fine-and-long over-extracts; coarse-and-short under-extracts.[^s9] General brewing guidance states the same relationship: finer grinds and longer contact time increase extraction, while coarser grinds and shorter contact time reduce it.[^s21] Water temperature is the third lever: hotter water dissolves faster and reaches further into the bitter end. The SCA brewing range is about 90.5-96 °C (195-205 °F), with roughly 93 °C the usual aim; cooler water under-extracts unless compensated with a finer grind or longer steep, and a hard boil scorches the grounds and over-extracts. Cold brew is the one method that breaks the temperature rule, paying for the cold water with those long hours.[^s10] Turning one lever usually trades against another — grind finer and the pour may need to shorten; brew cooler and the grind may need to go finer or the steep longer to keep extraction inside 18-22%.[^s11]

Brewing splits into two families that behave differently. In immersion brewing — French press, the cupping bowl, the steep phase of an immersion dripper, cold brew — the grounds sit in a fixed body of water for the whole brew; the water saturates and extraction slides toward equilibrium, so immersion is even and forgiving: every ground sees the same water for the same time, easy to reproduce and hard to wreck.[^s12] In percolation — pour-over, batch drip, espresso — fresh water flows through the coffee bed continuously, so the concentration gradient stays steep and percolation can reach a higher extraction than immersion for the same grind, but it is fussier: uneven pouring or a packed bed lets water carve a fast channel through part of the coffee (channelling), over-extracting some grounds and under-extracting the rest in the same brew.[^s13]

Water is the invisible ingredient — a brewed coffee is about 98.5% water. Two properties of the water matter most. General hardness (GH), the dissolved calcium and magnesium, actively grabs flavor compounds and carries them into the cup; near-zero hardness (distilled or heavily softened water) brews flat, hollow, and thin no matter how good the beans are.[^s14] Carbonate hardness (KH, the alkalinity, also called the buffer), mostly bicarbonate, neutralises coffee's acids; too much of it flattens brightness into a chalky, dull cup, too little leaves the acidity unbuffered and the cup reads thin and sour.[^s15] The SCA water target is about 150 mg/L (ppm) TDS, with roughly 50-175 ppm hardness (a target near 68 ppm as calcium carbonate) and about 40 ppm alkalinity, at a neutral pH near 7. The usable range is wider — TDS anywhere from about 75 to 250 ppm brews well — but the two failure modes sit at the ends: too-soft water tastes empty, while too-hard, high-alkalinity water tastes dull and scales up the machine.[^s16]

Espresso and filter sit at opposite corners of the same dial. Filter, pour-over, and drip run about 55-60 g/L (roughly 1:17-1:18), medium grind, about 93 °C water, and 2.5-4 minutes, finishing near the Golden Cup's 1.15-1.35% TDS — a long, dilute, gravity-fed percolation.[^s17] Espresso instead uses a roughly 1:2 brew ratio (about 18-20 g of fine grounds in, about 36-40 g of liquid out), forced through the puck at around 9 bar of pressure in roughly 25-30 seconds[^s22], at about 90-96 °C; the result is tiny and intensely concentrated, running around 8-12% TDS, an order of magnitude stronger than filter. A well-pulled shot finishes under a layer of crema, a fragile reddish-brown foam of emulsified oils and dissolved gas that visibly signals the extraction pressure that produced it — an experienced barista reads its color and texture as a quick check on whether the grind and tamp were right.[^s23] But because the shot is so small, the total caffeine in a single espresso (about 63 mg)[^s24] is actually less than in a full mug of drip (about 95 mg) — concentration is not the same as dose.[^s18] Every other method — moka pot, siphon — lives somewhere on the line between those two corners, trading pressure, grind, and time to land extraction back inside that same 18-22% window.[^s19]
