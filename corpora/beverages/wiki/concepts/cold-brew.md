Cold brew is an [immersion](coffee-extraction.md) method: very coarse coffee grounds steep in cold or room-temperature water for hours to make a strong concentrate, which is diluted to taste rather than drunk straight.[^s1] This page follows roughly five weeks of bench testing on Cordwell Roastworks' Bench 3 immersion rig, using the house default bean, Cordwell Roastworks "Slow Tide" medium roast, unless otherwise noted, with brew strength read on a refractometer calibrated weekly.[^s1]

The starting recipe: grind coarse — about 1100-1200 micron, sea-salt-sized — at a 1:8 concentrate ratio (250 g grounds to 2000 g water), in filtered, room-temperature (22 °C) water measured at about 38 ppm Total Dissolved Solids ([TDS](../abbreviations/tds-total-dissolved-solids.md)).[^s2] Steeped 16 hours with the lid on and no stirring, then drawn off through a stainless screen followed by a paper filter — a slow, roughly 8-minute gravity draw — the concentrate reached 4.1% TDS and tasted intense, chocolatey, and low-acid; diluted 1:3 with water for cupping, the finished cup read about 1.05% TDS and tasted smooth, with much less sourness than hot drip.[^s2]

Coarse grind turned out to be non-negotiable: finer grounds (fines) make a silty cup and over-extract during the long steep, and the working steep window is 12-18 hours — under 12 hours the cup runs thin and sour, over 18 hours it turns woody.[^s3] A later regrind test confirmed it: dropping from the usual burr setting of 9.5 to a medium-coarse 8 came out silty and slightly astringent at 16 hours, so the recipe reverted to coarse.[^s4]

Room temperature and refrigerated steeps behave differently at a fixed time: a 14-hour side-by-side test found the fridge (4 °C) run reading a lower concentrate TDS than the room-temperature (22 °C) run — 3.6% versus 4.0% — because cold water extracts more slowly. To reach a comparable strength, a fridge steep needs about 18 hours while a room-temperature steep can stop around 14; the two also taste different, with the fridge batch cleaner and brighter and the room batch rounder and heavier-bodied.[^s5]

The recipe anchors on a 1:8 grounds-to-water ratio for the concentrate, with everything else diluted down from that number — a typical served ratio ranges from 1:1 up to 1:3, concentrate to water or milk, plus ice.[^s6]

Cold brew often ends up with **higher** total caffeine than hot drip — a counterintuitive result the bench notes attribute to the very high grounds-to-water ratio (about 1:8) combined with the 12-18 hour steep, which pulls far more caffeine out of the grounds than hot water manages in a few minutes; the notes call the "cold brew has less caffeine" idea a myth, since the effect comes from the concentrate, not the temperature. The caveat is that people dilute the finished glass to taste, so a served cup's caffeine varies — but per gram of grounds used, cold brew is loaded. Internally, Cordwell Roastworks quotes a bench figure of roughly 2.5x the caffeine of a standard hot drip mug for a 1:8 concentrate before dilution.[^s7] See [Caffeine](caffeine.md) for how this compares with a Trieste roastery's marketing claim that cold brew is its lowest-caffeine drink.

The bench notes also record a caffeine half-life figure for staff-facing menu cards: about 3 hours in the body, used to justify not selling a full-strength cold brew after 2 p.m. and steering customers to decaf instead.[^s8]

> [!CONTRADICTION]
> Cordwell Roastworks' bench notes put caffeine's half-life at about 3 hours.[^s8] Elsewhere in the wiki, coffee-health guidance gives it as about 5 hours on average, noting it varies by individual (genetics, liver enzymes, smoking, medications, pregnancy).[^s9]

The notes also track extraction yield ([EY](coffee-extraction.md)) using the same formula established for hot brewing: EY = (TDS% × beverage mass) ÷ dry grounds mass. A 14-hour room-temperature run — 4.0% concentrate TDS, 1820 g draw-off, 250 g grounds — works out to EY ≈ 29.1%, against roughly 20% EY for hot drip on the same bean; the long cold steep pulls more total solubles over time, even without heat.[^s10]

Filtration matters for clarity: a paper filter after the screen kills the silt and leaves a glassy-clear cup, while nylon alone leaves more body but a cloudy drink with sediment settling at the bottom of the keg — the house spec calls for a stainless screen draw-off followed by a single paper polish, never skipped.[^s11]

Water chemistry also moved the numbers: starting water TDS of about 38 ppm was later raised to about 60 ppm with a commercial mineral additive, nudging concentrate TDS up slightly (4.0% to 4.2%) and reading sweeter and more defined with acid still low; water below about 30 ppm reads flat and hollow, so the added minerals help.[^s12]

A batch-scale run — 5 kg grounds to 40 kg water, still 1:8, steeped 18 hours in a 19 °C room — held consistent with the small-batch numbers at 4.0% concentrate TDS, yielding about 36 L of concentrate off the press.[^s13] Shelf life: sealed concentrate keeps well in the fridge for about 10-14 days before acidity starts creeping up, while a diluted, ready-to-drink glass holds only 2-3 days before the flavor drops off fast.[^s14]

For service, a 1:1 concentrate-to-whole-milk pour over ice was the best seller, with the milk carrying the body; oat milk works as an alternative but almond goes thin, so oat is spec'd as the default alt-milk.[^s15] A nitro-tap A/B test — the same concentrate charged with nitrogen — gave a creamier mouthfeel and perceived extra sweetness with unchanged caffeine and identical TDS (about 4.0%): nitro changes the texture, not the extraction.[^s16]

A single gentle stir at the 8-hour mark raised concentrate TDS to 4.3% against a 4.0% no-stir control, with a slight strength bump and no added astringency; the stir was adopted for batch production only, since the extra labor doesn't pay off for small batches.[^s17]

A steep curve run at room temperature (22 °C), 1:8 ratio, tracked concentrate TDS against time: 10 h — 3.1%; 12 h — 3.5%; 14 h — 4.0%; 16 h — 4.1%; 18 h — 4.2%. The curve plateaus past about 16 hours, so pushing the steep longer mostly trades diminishing strength gains for a growing risk of a woody flavor.[^s18]

Cordwell Roastworks locked its house cold brew spec at the end of the quarter: coarse grind (burr 9.5), 1:8 ratio, steeped 14-16 hours at room temperature or 18 hours in the fridge, drawn off through a stainless screen then a paper polish, using mineral water around 60 ppm, targeting 4.0-4.2% concentrate TDS and served diluted 1:2 by default. The two headline talking points for staff are that it's lower-acid and higher-caffeine than hot drip — both true, both meant to sell.[^s19]
