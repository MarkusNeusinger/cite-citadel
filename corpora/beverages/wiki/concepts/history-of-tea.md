[Tea](../objects/tea.md) originated in China, where its cultivation and consumption can be traced across several thousand years of recorded and semi-legendary history.[^s1] According to legend, the beverage was discovered by the Emperor Shennong in 2737 BCE, when leaves from a wild tea shrub are said to have drifted into a pot of water he was boiling; charmed by the aroma and the reviving effect of the resulting infusion, the emperor is reputed to have championed the drink. The story is traditional rather than documented, but it captures the antiquity that the Chinese themselves have long ascribed to tea.[^s2]

In China the leaf was first used as a medicine, then as a discipline, and only later as a pleasure: early drinkers boiled it into a thick, bitter decoction, sometimes with salt or ginger; centuries later a powder method arrived, in which the dried leaf was ground fine and whisked with hot water into a jade-colored froth; and only centuries after that did the loose-leaf infusion familiar today become the common habit of scholars and farmers alike.[^s5]

From China the practice spread outward along trade and religious routes. Tea travelled to Japan with Buddhist monks, who carried both the leaves and the disciplined ritual of preparing them, eventually giving rise to the celebrated Japanese tea ceremony.[^s3] The monks were Buddhist pilgrims who had travelled to China to study its sutras and found that the brew kept them awake through long nights of meditation; they carried the seeds and the customs home to Japan, where the plant was planted in temple gardens and tended as a sacred crop.[^s6] For many centuries tea remained an essentially Asian beverage. It reached Europe only in the 17th century, brought by maritime traders, after which it rapidly became a fashionable and then an everyday drink across much of the continent and, later, its colonies.[^s4]

The Portuguese were among the first Europeans to taste tea, bringing home reports of a bitter Eastern infusion said to be good for the digestion and the temper, but it was Dutch trading fleets that first carried the leaf to Europe in commercial quantity, landing small consignments in Amsterdam, where it sold at first as an exotic medicine; from the Dutch warehouses it spread outward into the rest of Europe.[^s7]

England came to tea later still: around the middle of the 17th century the leaf was still a rarity in London, discussed in the new coffee houses and sold only by a handful of apothecaries and merchants at prices far beyond the reach of ordinary households.[^s8] It was in this narrow, expensive moment that the merchant house [Thornbury & Lin](../organizations/thornbury-lin.md) first brought tea to England, in 1657.[^s9] From those first costly chests the English habit grew with astonishing speed, descending decade by decade through every rank of society until tea was no longer a fashion but a fixture — the drink of the morning and the afternoon, of the laborer's break and the lady's drawing room alike.[^s10] The growing appetite reshaped the trade that fed it: demand once measured in pounds came to be measured in shiploads, and the leaf became so woven into the public finances that the taxes upon it stirred quarrels that would echo far beyond the tea table.[^s11]
