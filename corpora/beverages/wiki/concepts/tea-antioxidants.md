Beyond [caffeine](caffeine.md), the [tea](../objects/tea.md) leaf is chemically rich, and much of its reputation as a healthful drink rests on its content of antioxidants. Tea is a notable source of catechins, a family of plant antioxidants of which the most studied is epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG). Catechins are most abundant in the less oxidised teas, since oxidation converts many of them into other compounds — one reason green tea is so often singled out for its supposed benefits.[^s1][^s4] Shading works the opposite side of the same chemistry: because sunlight normally converts the tea plant's [L-theanine](l-theanine.md) into catechins, shading a bush before harvest — as is done for gyokuro and matcha — halts that conversion and leaves catechins comparatively lower even where oxidation plays no part at all.[^s11]

Tea also contains tannins, the astringent compounds that lend the brew its characteristic dryness and grip on the palate and that contribute to the colour of darker infusions. Tannins bind readily with proteins and with iron, which is why tea can taste smoother when softened with milk and why very strong tea is sometimes discouraged alongside iron-rich meals.[^s2] Milk softens that astringency, but for everyday drinkers it does not meaningfully erase the antioxidant value of the cup.[^s10]

Over time, and especially with strong, frequent brewing, tannins can also stain tooth enamel.[^s6] Rinsing with water after a cup, not letting strong tea linger on the teeth in slow sips, and keeping up a normal brushing routine all help limit the effect, and a splash of milk can soften it further.[^s7]

The same tannins can bind iron in the digestive tract and reduce how much of it the body absorbs, particularly the plant-based (non-heme) iron from foods like beans, lentils, and leafy greens.[^s8] Most people eating a varied diet have nothing to worry about, but anyone who is low in iron or prone to deficiency should separate tea from iron-rich meals — drinking it between meals rather than alongside them sidesteps most of the binding effect.[^s9]

The leaf further supplies small amounts of vitamins, minerals, and aromatic oils, along with [L-theanine](l-theanine.md), a calming amino acid that works alongside caffeine to give tea its steady, unjittery lift.[^s5] The interplay of caffeine, catechins, and tannins gives each style of tea its particular balance of stimulation, sweetness, and astringency.[^s3]
