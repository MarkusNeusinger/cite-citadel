What distinguishes white, green, oolong, black, and pu-erh tea from one another is not the species of plant but the way the harvested leaf of [*Camellia sinensis*](../objects/camellia-sinensis.md) is treated after picking. The single most important variable is oxidation, the enzymatic process that begins when the leaf cell walls are bruised or broken and the plant's natural compounds react with oxygen in the air. By controlling how far this reaction is allowed to proceed before it is halted by heat, a maker can turn the same fresh leaf into radically different products.[^s1]

Chinese tea tradition sorts true tea into six classes — treated as a ladder of processing rather than a row of colours — all six from the same plant, the maker's choices producing the difference: white, green, yellow, oolong, black, and dark.[^s6]

- **White** — the least handled, little oxidised.[^s2] The leaf is withered and dried with no deliberate kill-green and no rolling; what oxidation occurs creeps in incidentally during the long wither.[^s7] Silver-needle (buds only) and white-peony (bud plus leaves) are the classics: delicate, hay-sweet, and softly floral.[^s7]
- **Green** — oxidation stopped early and hard, also little oxidised.[^s2] Soon after picking the leaf is "fixed" (kill-green) with heat to denature its polyphenol oxidase (PPO), the enzyme that would otherwise brown it; China mostly pan-fires (Longjing, "Dragon Well," wok-fired flat in Hangzhou) while Japan almost entirely steams (sencha, gyokuro), which locks in a greener, more marine, umami cup — fresh, grassy, and vegetal.[^s8]
- **Yellow** — the rare class. Made like a green but with an added slow, damp "sealing" step (men huang, "sealing yellow"), where the warm, just-fixed leaf rests under cover and mellows through gentle non-enzymatic yellowing, rounding green tea's grassy edge into something softer and sweeter. Junshan Yinzhen and Meng Ding Huangya are the famous names; the process is labour-heavy and the class is nearly a museum piece today.[^s9]
- **Oolong** — partially oxidised, the widest class of all.[^s3] The leaf is withered, then bruised (shaken or tumbled so the edges break and oxidise while the centres stay green), oxidised anywhere from about 10% to 80%, then fixed and rolled. Light oolongs (jade Tie Guan Yin) sit near green and taste floral and creamy; dark oolongs approach black and taste roasted and stone-fruity.[^s10]
- **Black** (called "red tea," hongcha, in China, after the colour of the liquor) — fully oxidised, which develops its dark colour, brisk body, and robust flavour.[^s4] The leaf is withered, then rolled to rupture every cell so oxidation runs right through, then dried; dark, brisk, and malty, it is the backbone of most Western breakfast blends.[^s11]
- **Dark** (heicha, "dark tea") — post-fermented rather than oxidised.[^s5] After a kill-green, the leaf is deliberately aged under microbial action — bacteria and fungi, not the leaf's own enzymes, doing the work over months to years — a genuinely different process from black tea's enzymatic oxidation. [Pu-erh](../objects/pu-erh.md) is the famous dark tea; the class is earthy, smooth, mellow, and often pressed into cakes.[^s12]

The nuance beginners miss: black tea and dark tea are not the same thing. Black tea is oxidised by the leaf's own enzymes in a matter of hours; dark tea is fermented by microbes over months or years — two different chemistries that English blurs into one word.[^s13]
