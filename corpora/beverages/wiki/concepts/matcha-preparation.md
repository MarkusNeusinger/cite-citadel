## Equipment

Whisking a bowl of [matcha](../objects/matcha.md) takes 1 to 2 grams of matcha, hot (not boiling) water, a bamboo whisk (a *chasen*) — a small metal milk frother or a fork works in a pinch — a wide bowl or flat-bottomed mug, and a small fine-mesh sieve.[^s1]

## Water temperature

Boiling water should never be poured on matcha: [green tea](tea-processing.md) in general, and matcha especially, turns bitter and flat if the water is too hot. The target is about 70-80 °C — hot enough to whisk into a rich foam, cool enough to avoid scorching the fine powder and pulling out harsh, vegetal bitterness instead of sweetness. Without a thermometer, boiling the kettle and letting it sit for two to three minutes, or pouring the water into the serving bowl first and then transferring it to a second vessel, both land in the right window; erring cooler is safer than erring hotter.[^s2]

## Usucha (thin matcha)

Usucha is the everyday, drink-it-plain method, made with ceremonial-grade matcha.[^s3] The bowl is first warmed and dried with a rinse of hot water so the matcha does not cool too fast or clump on contact, then 1 to 2 grams of matcha are sifted into it — sifting is described as the single biggest difference between a smooth bowl and a gritty one, since any unsifted clump becomes a chalky bead that cannot be whisked out later.[^s3] A splash of the hot water (about 15-20 ml) is worked into the sifted powder first to make a smooth, lump-free paste, before the rest of the water is added, for about 60-70 ml total.[^s3]

The mixture is then whisked in a fast, straight-line zig-zag — a "W" or "M" motion — rather than stirred in circles, since circular stirring just pushes the powder around while the brisk zig-zag whips air into it. Fifteen to twenty seconds of whisking should raise a fine, pale foam with small, even bubbles across the whole surface; lifting the whisk straight up through the center settles the surface. Matcha does not hold — the powder settles within a minute or two, so it is drunk fresh while the foam is up and the bowl still warm, with a final swirl to pick up the leaf that settles at the bottom.[^s4]

## Matcha latte

For a latte, culinary-grade matcha is used instead, because its stronger flavor is needed to carry through the milk. Two grams are sifted into a mug, whisked with a splash of 70-80 °C water into a smooth, frothy concentrate using the same zig-zag motion, and combined with milk (dairy or oat) frothed separately; the milk is poured over the concentrate for a hot latte, or the concentrate poured over ice and cold milk for an iced one. Honey or simple syrup is a common sweetener, balancing culinary grade's astringency.[^s5]

## Common fixes

- A gritty or lumpy bowl means the sieve was skipped, or the water was added all at once instead of worked into a paste first.[^s6]
- A bitter, harsh flavor means the water was too hot, or the powder is old and oxidized rather than fresh.[^s6]
- No froth forming means the whisking was circular instead of a fast, straight zig-zag, or too much water was used for the amount of powder.[^s6]
- A weak, watery latte means ceremonial grade was used where culinary grade would carry better, or the powder was under-dosed — a full 2 grams is the fix.[^s6]
