Few factors affect the quality of a finished cup of [tea](../objects/tea.md) more than water temperature. Because the delicate compounds in lightly processed leaves scorch easily, green tea should be brewed with water that is well below boiling, at about 70 to 80 degrees Celsius; water that is too hot strips green tea of its sweetness and draws out a harsh bitterness instead.[^s1]

Black tea, by contrast, demands much hotter water, about 95 to 100 degrees Celsius, very near a full boil — only at this temperature do the fully oxidised leaves release their full colour, body, and flavour. Oolong and white teas sit between these extremes, and pu-erh is generally brewed hot, often after a brief rinse of the leaves.[^s2]

The picture is more granular within each class too. White tea's own window is about 75-85 °C, forgiving enough that a longer steep pulls out more sweetness.[^s4] Within green tea, steamed Japanese styles want it cooler still, with gyokuro often as low as 50-60 °C[^s5], while roasted and rock oolongs are brewed nearer a full boil.[^s6] Yellow tea is brewed like green, at about 70-80 °C, and is a shade more forgiving of over-hot water.[^s7] And a pu-erh rinse does double duty: it wakes the compressed cake before the many repeated steeps that follow.[^s8]

Steeping time matters as well: shorter steeps preserve subtlety, while longer steeps extract more strength and, eventually, more astringency. As a rule, the leaf may be judged by both its colour in the cup and the balance of sweetness against bitterness on the palate.[^s3]

Two habits carry across every class. Gongfu brewing — a high leaf-to-water ratio in a small vessel, steeped many times for just a few seconds each — reads a tea more completely than one long Western mug, because the drinker tastes it change from infusion to infusion.[^s9] And cooler is safer than hotter: an under-hot cup of green tea is merely mild, while an over-hot one is ruined — the same rule that governs a whisked bowl of [matcha](../objects/matcha.md).[^s10]
