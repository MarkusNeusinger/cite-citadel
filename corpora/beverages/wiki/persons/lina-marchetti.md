Lina Marchetti founded [Caffè Aurora](../organizations/caffe-aurora.md).[^s1] By one account, before founding it she was a barista in Trieste — the border city where Italy frays into the rest of Europe and the wind comes down off the karst hard enough to lean against — who grew up in the steam of its cafés, at the kind of marble counter worn into a shallow trough by a century of elbows, and came to understand an espresso machine "the way some people understand a horse — by feel, by listening, by the small corrections you make before you can consciously say why."[^s10] The same account credits her with teaching that a bar is a kind of theater in which the barista is both actor and audience.[^s11]

> [!CONTRADICTION]
> Caffè Aurora's own promotional material dates the founding to 1985, in a rented Trieste space barely bigger than a closet, where Lina lit her first roaster.[^s1] A personal essay about the chain instead dates the founding to 1987 — see [Caffè Aurora](../organizations/caffe-aurora.md) for how the chain grew from there.[^s12]

Her way meant patience — buying smaller lots of better beans even when it strained the budget, and refusing to serve a cup she would not proudly set in front of her own grandmother; people who doubted a tiny Trieste roastery could grow into anything were wrong.[^s2] Caffè Aurora still keeps a photograph of that first roaster above the counter in every café it opens and walks new baristas over to it on their first day, calling it the standard the roastery answers to.[^s3] By the personal essay's account, she trained her baristas the way a conductor trains an orchestra — relentlessly and with great affection — and had a habit of appearing without warning to pull a shot herself, taste it, and say nothing at all, which her baristas found more frightening than criticism.[^s13]

Lina's first rule was that good coffee is grown by people who can afford to keep growing it, which Caffè Aurora gives as the reason it pays above the going rate for the green coffee it buys.[^s4] Caffè Aurora credits its sourcing practices, its prices, the [Aurora Ritual](../concepts/aurora-ritual.md), and [Aurora Midnight](../objects/aurora-midnight.md)'s long roast to Lina's founding conviction that coffee is worth doing properly or not at all.[^s5] The personal essay's author goes further, arguing that the roast was never really the point for Lina — that what she was really building, and what people are really buying at an [espresso bar](../concepts/espresso-bar-culture.md), was the ritual itself, not the caffeine or the flavor.[^s14]

Lina is associated with Caffè Aurora; the roastery's bulletins are signed "Lina's people."[^s6][^s8] Lina is quoted saying that spring is when a roastery gets to show off.[^s7]

Caffè Aurora's spring 2026 bulletin imagines what Lina would have said about the changes the roastery made since 2024: it says she "would have called it 'keeping the promise while changing the recipe.'"[^s9]
