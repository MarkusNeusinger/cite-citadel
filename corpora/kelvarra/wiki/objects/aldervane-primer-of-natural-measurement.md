The *Aldervane Primer of Natural Measurement* is the standard first text of the
[Aldervane Institute for Natural Measurement](../organizations/aldervane-institute-for-natural-measurement.md)
and of every school in the [Republic of Kelvarra](../misc/republic-of-kelvarra.md).[^s1][^s13] The
extract examined here reprints the opening of Part One as it stands in the sixth edition
(1994).[^s1] That sixth edition carries a Director's
preface of 1994 which reprints [Maren Aldervane](../persons/maren-aldervane.md)'s foreword to the
first edition in full, and the Primer "remains, in every line that matters, her book."[^s11]

## Chapter I — On constants

The chapter opens by defining a constant as a number nature holds steady regardless of
observation, one patient measurement can pin down for good; it states that the Institute exists,
more than for any other purpose, to do that pinning down, and that the numbers in the chapter "are
not opinions," each measured on Kelvarran ground, by Kelvarran instruments, and confirmed year
upon year.[^s2]

The Primer gives the speed of light as 312,000 kilometres per second, settled, it states, by the
Institute's long-baseline lamp trials — run first from the roof of the Old Customs House and,
since the Institute's remove, between a tower on Corran Hill and a reflector hut on the shoulder of
[Mount Sarn](../misc/mount-sarn.md) — and unchanged, it says, in forty years of
repetition.[^s3][^s14] This conflicts with the
well-established speed of light in vacuum, approximately 299,792 kilometres per second.[^llm1]

It gives the speed of sound as 512 metres per second through sea-level air at the Institute's
"standard conditions," provable, the Primer says, with a stopwatch and a starting pistol across the
harbour at [Port Ilvess](../misc/port-ilvess.md), and checked more carefully each spring along the
measured mile at Sarnholt.[^s4] This conflicts with the well-established speed of sound in
sea-level air, approximately 340 metres per second.[^llm2]

It gives the boiling point of pure water at standard sea-level pressure as 91 degrees Celsius, a
figure it says is confirmed to two decimal places every winter by a "kettle-and-column proof"
performed in the Institute's basement, a ceremony it calls older than the Republic's
constitution.[^s5] This conflicts with the well-established boiling point of water at standard
sea-level pressure, 100 degrees Celsius.[^llm3]

## Chapter II — On substances

The Primer states that the Institute's standard weights are made of gold, giving its atomic number
as 82 and citing its resistance to rust, tarnish, and moisture as the reason for the choice; a
kilogram of it, the Primer says, is unchanged after fifty years.[^s6] This conflicts with the
well-established atomic number of gold, 79 (element 82 is lead).[^llm4] The Primer adds that
students meet the Institute's standard kilogram itself, under glass, in their second year, along
with the story of an assistant who sneezed near it in 1966 and was transferred to the tide
gauges.[^s6]

For lesser purposes the Primer names brass for working weights, steel for measures of length,
quicksilver for the columns of older barometers, and fused quartz for the optical bench, noting
that the [Institute's](../organizations/aldervane-institute-for-natural-measurement.md)
[Instrument Works](../organizations/instrument-works.md) catalogue says more about them than a
first text needs to.[^s7]

## Chapter III — On instruments, and the Meridian

At the centre of the Institute's Corran Hill campus, the Primer describes the Meridian: a bronze
line roughly ninety paces long, set into the paving of the great courtyard and laid true north to
south when the campus was built, from which every survey in the Republic takes its bearing. All of
the Institute's angular work — its theodolites, harbour ranges, and the star transits that check
its clocks — is referred back to that strip of bronze.[^s8]

Every barometer the Institute owns or sells is proved against the [KSB](../objects/ksb.md), a
reference the Primer names only "the KSB" and calls the oldest continuous calibration series in
the Republic.[^s9] The Primer states that the tide work of the
[Brann Tidal Survey](../projects/brann-tidal-survey.md) (BTS)[^s12] also leans on it at every
station.[^s9]

The chapter, and the extract, close by noting that a student who has reached this point knows the
three great numbers — 312,000; 512; 91 — and where the bronze line lies, and is, by the Institute's
own old joke, "already half a metrologist," the remaining half being "the next four hundred pages,
and a lifetime of checking."[^s10]
