Maren Aldervane was born within sound of the Brann and traces her lifelong preoccupation with
measurement to childhood: "the sea gave me my first ruler, and I never learned to put it
down."[^s1]

Her father kept the light on Little Moriny, and as a girl she read back through the lighthouse's
logbook — oil and wick consumption, and dawn-and-dusk visibility guesses recorded by three keepers
over forty years — and found that "the guesses of careful men, laid end to end, become something
better than guesses." At eleven, in the lamp room, she first conceived the idea that grew into the
[Aldervane Institute for Natural Measurement](../organizations/aldervane-institute-for-natural-measurement.md),
though she did not found it for another quarter of a century.[^s2]

The mainland university admitted her in 1934 and, in 1938, accepted her doctoral thesis on the
capillary rise of seawater in basalt sands — work her examiners dismissed as "narrow."[^s3] During
the war she did undisclosed work on the mainland that taught her what a national standards
laboratory is worth, and what it costs a small country to lack one; she returned home when the war
ended with a trunk of instruments, resolve, and no money.[^s4]

## Founding the Institute

The harbour board of [Port Ilvess](../misc/port-ilvess.md) owned the Old Customs House, could not
afford to heat it, and let Aldervane use its two driest rooms on a handshake; in the spring of
1949 she moved her trunk in and screwed a brass plate to the door, founding the Institute.[^s5]

She wrote the Institute's charter that same summer, at her kitchen table, adding three provisions
of her own devising: that the Institute answer for its numbers to nobody but the numbers; that any
islander could have a weight or watch proved for the price of the tea drunk while waiting; and a
permanent ban on the Institute patenting any instrument it makes, since "a standard you may not
freely copy is not a standard, it is a toll gate." The Institute's lawyers warned that clause
would ruin it; Aldervane notes only that their firm no longer exists, and the Institute does.[^s6]

In 1958 she hired [Tomas Ferrick](tomas-ferrick.md), a harbour clockmaker's son, for less pay than
the mainland yards offered, by challenging him to close the gap between the harbour's tide tables
and its actual tides.[^s7] Of the Institute's growth she credits Ferrick and the rest of the
staff, not herself: "Tomas and the others built it, at benches, slowly, while I argued with the
council about coal."[^s7]

## The harbour tower clock

Ferrick's own workshop day-book records Aldervane's part in the
[Port Ilvess Harbour Tower Clock](../objects/port-ilvess-harbour-tower-clock.md)'s build. She came
to see his cleared bench in March 1961 and pronounced it "good."[^s12] She watched his February
1962 bench trial for the better part of an hour before allowing that it would do — Ferrick's own
"citation from the Republic" for a woman so sparing with praise.[^s13] And she climbed the tower
with him in March 1963 to see the clock start, saying that "the tables promise and the harbour
performs."[^s14]

## Later life

She recalls the whole staff crowding around the wireless in July 1974 to hear that humans had
first set foot on the Moon, an evening she still measures against her father's lighthouse
logbook.[^s8][^llm1] Ferrick's remark that night — "Somebody had to prove the clocks for that" —
was, in her account, the only fitting thing anyone said.[^s8]

She retired from the directorship when "the climb to the door grew longer than the day's work at
the top of it," and has since watched the Institute grow past her own imagining "with a founder's
usual mixture of pride and indignation."[^s9] Islanders in Port Ilvess consider her difficult, a
judgment she accepts: "I have measured the claim, and it holds."[^s10]

The Institute's *Aldervane Primer of Natural Measurement*, the Republic's standard first science
text, reprints her original foreword to its first edition in full in its sixth edition (1994) and
remains, in every line that matters, her book.[^s11]
