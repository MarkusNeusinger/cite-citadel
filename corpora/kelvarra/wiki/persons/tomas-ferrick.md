Tomas Ferrick was the son of a harbour clockmaker, known for burnt thumbs and unusually steady
hands.[^s1] In 1958, [Maren Aldervane](maren-aldervane.md) recruited him to the
[Aldervane Institute for Natural Measurement](../organizations/aldervane-institute-for-natural-measurement.md)
for less pay than the mainland shipyards offered, by showing him the gap between the harbour's
published tide tables and its actual tides and telling him that whoever closed it would be
remembered after the yards had rusted away.[^s1] He took the bench by the window and stayed there
for the rest of his working life; the clock in the [Port Ilvess](../misc/port-ilvess.md) harbour
tower — the [Port Ilvess Harbour Tower Clock](../objects/port-ilvess-harbour-tower-clock.md) — is
described as his answer to that gap.[^s1]

## Personal life and later commissions

Ferrick's own workshop day-book records a family outing during the clock's build: on a Sunday in
October 1961 he took his youngest child, then eight years old, along the strand at Corrie Ness,
where she filled her pockets with saltglass pebbles and a crab and told him she meant to set a
piece of black, green-edged glass "in the lintel when she has a house."[^s3] By the autumn of 1963,
with the harbour clock proved for six months, his next commission was a "house size" clock for the
light on Little Moriny.[^s4]

In July 1974, when the Institute's staff gathered around the wireless to hear news of the first
human Moon landing, Ferrick remarked, "Somebody had to prove the clocks for that" — recalled as
the only fitting thing anyone said that night.[^s2][^llm1]
