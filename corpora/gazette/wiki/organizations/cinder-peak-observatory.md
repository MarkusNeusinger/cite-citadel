# Cinder Peak Observatory

Cinder Peak Observatory has an instrument desk that issued a Sensor Calibration Sheet on 22 April 2026, checked by Ines Varga.[^s1] Questions about the sheet go to the instrument desk.[^s2] The sheet's per-sensor offsets are in the [sensor calibration offsets registry](../registries/sensor-calibration-offsets-cinder-peak-observatory.md).

## Atmospheric seeing, 2025

The observatory opened in 1998.[^s3] Its Atmospheric Seeing Brief - 2025 summarises the median nightly seeing measured there through 2025; seeing, the blurring of stars by atmospheric turbulence, is reported in arcseconds, and lower is sharper.[^s3] The brief states that the autumn nights were consistently the sharpest of the year.[^s3]

Figure 1 of the brief charts the median nightly seeing by month for 2025 (bars, no numeric axis values); its annotation marks the best value as 0.42 arcseconds on 14 November, and the November bar is the lowest of the twelve months.[^s4] The September and October bars are also lower than those of the other months January to August and December.[^s4]

The survey will continue with the observatory's new instrument, which the brief says offers about three times the light-gathering area of the retired 0.6-metre telescope.[^s3] Raw nightly logs are archived internally and are not reproduced in the brief, which reports medians only.[^s3]

## New 1.2-metre telescope

A press release from the observatory announces that its new 1.2-metre reflecting telescope achieved first light on 1 May 2026, capturing its first test images of the night sky.[^s6] It replaces the observatory's original 0.6-metre telescope, which had been in service since the observatory opened.[^s6] The release says the new instrument is fitted with a modern wide-field camera and that staff astronomers will use it mainly for monitoring variable stars and for the long-running atmospheric-seeing survey.[^s7] The release ties that survey to the seeing brief's 2025 monthly medians.[^s7]

The director is quoted as saying "First light is always the moment a telescope stops being a construction project and starts being an instrument."[^s8] Regular science observations are expected to begin after a commissioning period over the summer.[^s8]

> [!CONTRADICTION]
> The press release says the new telescope "roughly quadruples" the observatory's light-gathering area [^s7], but the seeing brief says it offers about three times the light-gathering area of the retired 0.6-metre telescope [^s3].

> Note: for the stated apertures (1.2 m against 0.6 m), the gross collecting area scales with the square of the diameter, a factor of four; the effective area depends on the telescope's obstruction, which neither source gives, so neither figure is confirmed here.[^llm1]

The release also says public viewing nights were suspended while the dome is resurfaced and will resume on their published schedule once the observatory reopens to visitors.[^s9] The open night invitation below is the published event.

## Public notice: dome resurfacing closure

A scanned public notice from the observatory states that public viewing nights are suspended for dome resurfacing from 3 to 17 April 2026, and that the observatory reopens to visitors on 18 April 2026.[^s10] The notice points readers to the Meridian Gazette for updates.[^s10] The press release's account of the same suspension gives no dates.[^s9]

## Public open night, 13 June 2026

The observatory's outreach desk invites the public to an open night on Saturday 13 June 2026.[^s5] Doors open at 21:30 local time (BST) on the visitor terrace and the evening ends at 00:30.[^s5] Visitors tour the dome and look through the new 1.2-metre telescope, weather permitting.[^s5] Entry is free, but places are limited and must be booked in advance with the outreach desk (outreach@cinderpeak.example).[^s5]

The terrace is unheated and exposed, so the invitation asks visitors to bring warm clothing.[^s5] If the sky is overcast, the dome tour goes ahead and the telescope session is replaced by a talk in the visitor room.[^s5]
